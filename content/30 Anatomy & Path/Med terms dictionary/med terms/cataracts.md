>[!Note] DEFINITION of [[cataracts]]
>They develop slowly with **age, trauma, radiation, or diseases like [[diabetes]], leading to blindness if untreated**; surgery replaces the lens with an artificial one, restoring sight effectively.
>- **[[Nuclear sclerotic]]**: Central lens hardening.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cataract)​
>- **[[Cortical]]**: Wedge-like opacities.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cataract)​
>- **Posterior [[subcapsular]]**: Back lens clouding, glare-prone.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cataract)​
>- **[[Phacoemulsification]]**: Ultrasonic lens removal surgery.[](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cataract)​
>- **[[Glaucoma]]**: Often co-occurs, pressure-related damage.
> ---
> 
> **[[Cataracts]]** are more than just “**cloudy vision**.” They’re a gradual clouding of the eye’s natural lens that can c**ause glare, faded colors, halos around lights, trouble driving at night, and frequent prescription changes**; **cataracts** are usually not painful and may affect far and near vision differently depending on the type.
> 
> ## **What happens inside the eye**
> 
> The **lens** sits behind the iris and helps focus light onto the **retina**. With **cataracts**, proteins in the lens clump together and the lens becomes less transparent, so light scatters instead of focusing cleanly.
> 
> That’s why patients often describe **vision as looking through a fogged window.** The change can be slow, and some people adapt at first without realizing how much they’ve compensated.
> 
> ##### **Common symptom patterns**
> 
> **Different cataract types** tend to cause different visual complaints. **Nuclear cataracts** often affect distance vision first and can make the lens yellow or brown, which can also make colors harder to distinguish.[](https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/cataracts/symptoms-causes/syc-20353790)
> 
> **Cortical cataracts** often cause glare and trouble in **bright light, while posterior subcapsular cataracts often hit near vision, distance vision**, and glare sensitivity more quickly than the others.
> 
> 
> ## **Risk factors and causes**
> 
> **Age** is the most common risk factor, but cataracts can also be associated **with trauma, radiation exposure, diabetes, smoking, and steroid use**.
> 
> Because several of these factors overlap with other **eye diseases**, it is common for **[[cataracts]]** and **[[glaucoma]]** to coexist in the same patient.
> 
> ## **Non-surgical management**
> 
> There is no proven medicine or eye drop that reliably reverses cataracts. Early on, symptoms may be managed with updated **glasses, better lighting, anti-glare lenses, magnification, UV protection, and smoking cessation**.
> 
> These measures can help function, but they do not remove the cloudy lens. They mainly **buy time** until surgery is needed.
> 
> ## **Surgery and recovery**
> 
> When **cataracts** start interfering with daily life, surgery is the definitive treatment. The **cloudy lens** is removed and replaced with an artificial **intraocular lens**, and **[[phacoemulsification]]** is the common technique used to break up and remove the lens.
> 
> **Outcomes** are generally excellent, with vision often improving significantly after surgery. The exact timing depends on how much the **cataract affects reading, driving, work, and safety**.
